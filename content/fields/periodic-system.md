core_question: Why do the elements, arranged by weight, fall into repeating families — and can the pattern be trusted to predict elements nobody has seen?

summary: |-
  Once there was one agreed set of atomic weights, a question that had been unanswerable became urgent: is the order meaningful? Several people noticed that listing the elements by increasing weight brings chemically similar ones round at regular intervals, and at least five published versions of the idea in the 1860s. What distinguishes Dmitri Mendeleev's table of 1869 is not the arrangement but what he was willing to do with it. He left gaps where the pattern demanded an element that was not known, predicted the properties of three of them in detail, and corrected measured atomic weights that disagreed with the position he thought an element should hold.

  Within fifteen years gallium, scandium and germanium had been isolated and matched the predictions closely enough to settle the matter. The pattern was then nearly broken by a group the table had no room for — the noble gases, discovered from 1894 — and finally explained by something outside chemistry: Henry Moseley's demonstration in 1913 that the ordering principle is not weight but nuclear charge, a whole number that says exactly how many elements can exist between hydrogen and uranium, and how many of them were still missing.

key_ideas:
  - term: Periodicity
    definition: >-
      Arrange the elements in order and chemical similarity recurs at intervals — the halogens
      together, the alkali metals together — so the list is better drawn as a table, with families in
      columns.
    turning_point_id: newlands-law-of-octaves
  - term: Predictive gap
    definition: >-
      A blank left in the table on the grounds that the pattern requires an element there. It
      converts a classification into a theory, because the blank carries predicted properties and can
      be wrong.
    turning_point_id: eka-element-predictions
  - term: Group and period
    definition: >-
      A column of chemically similar elements and a row across which properties change steadily.
      Position in both fixes an element's expected valency, oxide formula, density and volatility
      closely enough to interpolate.
    turning_point_id: mendeleev-periodic-table
  - term: Atomic number
    definition: >-
      The count of positive charges in the nucleus, which Moseley showed can be read off an element's
      X-ray spectrum. It orders the elements without exception, where weight has three inversions,
      and it is an integer, so the number of elements in a range is fixed.
    turning_point_id: moseley-atomic-number
  - term: Inner transition series
    definition: >-
      A block in which an inner shell fills while the outer one does not, so the elements are
      chemically almost indistinguishable — the lanthanides, and, once Seaborg moved them, the
      actinides.
    turning_point_id: seaborg-actinide-concept

## The Pattern Several People Saw

Karlsruhe left chemistry with one agreed table of atomic weights, and within a decade at least five people had noticed the same thing about it. List the elements by increasing weight and chemical character returns periodically: lithium, sodium and potassium at intervals; fluorine, chlorine and bromine likewise.

{{fig:newlands|John Newlands}} published a version in 1865 and called it the law of octaves, by analogy with music. When he presented it to the Chemical Society the following year, a Fellow asked whether he had considered arranging the elements alphabetically, and the Society declined to publish the paper. Alexandre Béguyer de Chancourtois had drawn the elements on a spiral round a cylinder in 1862; William Odling's 1864 table is close to the one now in every classroom.

{{fig:mendeleev|Dmitri Mendeleev}} came to it in 1869 through a textbook — he needed an order in which to present the elements to students — and his table was not notably better than Odling's. What he did with it was different in kind. He left gaps, asserting that elements must exist to fill them. He put tellurium before iodine although tellurium weighs more, because their chemistry demanded it. And he revised published atomic weights that conflicted with the position he thought an element should occupy: beryllium from 13.5 to 9, indium, uranium from 120 to 240. Those were measurements made by competent people, overruled by a pattern.

{{fig:lothar-meyer|Lothar Meyer}} had the same arrangement independently, and published a graph of atomic volume against weight in 1864 that shows the periodicity more vividly than any table. The two later argued about priority. The conventional verdict — Meyer found it, Mendeleev bet on it — is roughly right and flatters both of them, since the arrangement was in the air.

## A Family With No Column

The table's sternest test came from an element nobody had looked for, and it was found because of a discrepancy in the fourth decimal place.

{{fig:rayleigh|Lord Rayleigh}} had been measuring gas densities carefully for years when he noticed that nitrogen prepared from the air was consistently denser than nitrogen prepared from ammonia — by about half a per cent, reproducibly, well beyond his errors. With {{fig:ramsay|William Ramsay}} he set about removing everything known from a sample of air, burning away the oxygen and absorbing the nitrogen with hot magnesium, and found a residue of about one per cent of the original volume that would react with nothing at all. They named it argon, from the Greek for idle.

An element that forms no compounds is awkward in a system built on valency, and the table had no column for it. {{fig:mendeleev|Mendeleev}} initially doubted that it was an element, suggesting it might be a triatomic form of nitrogen. Within four years Ramsay had found helium in a uranium mineral, and then neon, krypton and xenon by fractionating liquid air — a whole family, all unreactive, with atomic weights that placed them between the halogens and the alkali metals.

The outcome is the strongest evidence the periodic law received. Inserting an entire new group *between* two existing ones left the rest of the arrangement untouched: every other element kept its place, every other family stayed intact, and the period lengths went from 8 to 8 with a new member each. A classification that can absorb eight unforeseen elements without rearrangement is describing something real. It also sharpened the question the table could not answer — why periods are the length they are — because the noble gases sit exactly where a shell closes, which is a fact about electrons that nothing in 1898 could have supplied.

## A Closer Look: Predicting Germanium Fifteen Years Early

Mendeleev's 1871 paper describes three missing elements. Take the one under silicon, which he called eka-silicon.

**The interpolation.** Its neighbours in the table are known: gallium at 69.72 and arsenic at 74.92 on the same row, silicon at 28.09 and tin at 118.71 in the same column. The simplest estimate of its weight is the mean of its horizontal neighbours:

$$
\frac{69.72 + 74.92}{2} = 72.32.
$$

Germanium's atomic weight is 72.63. A mean of two numbers, taken before the element existed, lands within half a per cent.

Density is harder, because density does not interpolate linearly — but *atomic volume*, the atomic weight divided by the density, varies smoothly across the table, and that was Meyer's observation. Mendeleev predicted an atomic volume of 13, hence a density of $72/13 = 5.5$ g/cm³.

**The result.** Clemens Winkler isolated the element from argyrodite in 1886 and named it germanium:

| Property | Predicted, 1871 | Found, 1886 |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic weight | 72 | 72.3 |
| Density | 5.5 g/cm³ | 5.47 g/cm³ |
| Atomic volume | 13 | 13.2 |
| Oxide | $\mathrm{EsO_2}$, density 4.7 | $\mathrm{GeO_2}$, density 4.70 |
| Chloride | boils below 100 °C, density 1.9 | $\mathrm{GeCl_4}$ boils at 86 °C, density 1.89 |

Every entry agrees to within about a per cent. Check the consistency: $72.3 / 5.47 = 13.2$, which is the atomic volume, exactly as the interpolation required.

This is not what a classification does. A classification sorts what is already in hand; this made five quantitative statements about a substance nobody possessed, any one of which could have failed. Gallium in 1875 came close to failing: {{fig:lecoq|Lecoq de Boisbaudran}} measured its density as 4.7 against a prediction of 5.9, and Mendeleev wrote to tell him his sample was contaminated. On repurification it came out at 5.9.

**What the predictions cost.** It is worth recording the failures too, because the atlas's business is what a method can and cannot do. Mendeleev predicted elements lighter than hydrogen — coronium, and an "element zero" filling the role of the ether — which do not exist. He initially refused to accept argon, suggesting it was a triatomic form of nitrogen, because it fitted nowhere. And the pattern he was predicting *from* had no explanation at all: nothing in the table said why periods should be eight long, or why the length should change further down. A method can be reliable and unexplained at the same time, and for forty-four years this one was.

## What It Was Really Ordered By

The explanation came from physics, and it arrived in two parts.

The first was a correction to the ordering itself. {{fig:moseley|Henry Moseley}} bombarded element after element with electrons and measured the frequency of the characteristic X-rays each emitted. Plotting the square root of frequency against position gave a straight line with equal steps, one per element, and the step corresponds to one unit of positive nuclear charge. So the ordering principle is an integer — the atomic number — and not the weight. All three of the inversions that had embarrassed the table disappear, because tellurium has a lower atomic number than iodine while weighing more. The integer also did something the weights could not: it said how many elements can lie between two known ones, so the remaining gaps could be counted rather than guessed. Seven were outstanding between hydrogen and uranium; all seven were eventually found or made.

The second part was the shell structure of the atom, which says why periods have the lengths they do — and that belongs to [old quantum theory](/physics/old-quantum-theory/) and to [chemical bonding](/chemistry/chemical-bonding/).

The table has been altered since, most consequentially by {{fig:seaborg|Glenn Seaborg}} in 1944. Thorium and uranium sat among the transition metals, and the new transuranic elements made in the Manhattan Project behaved wrongly there. Seaborg proposed that a second inner series starts at actinium, parallel to the lanthanides — a rearrangement that predicted the oxidation states of the next several elements and told their makers what chemistry to use to separate them. At the bottom of that block the periodic law now runs into its own limit: in elements that exist a few atoms at a time, inner electrons move fast enough for relativity to reorder the orbitals, and whether an element's chemistry still follows its column is the open question above.
