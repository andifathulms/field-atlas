core_question: Why are the elements distributed through the Earth as they are, and what can their proportions tell you about the planet's history?

summary: |-
  Frank Clarke spent thirty years analysing rocks for the United States Geological Survey and published the result as a table: eight elements make up about ninety-eight per cent of the Earth's crust by mass, and everything else — including every metal industry depends on — is in the remaining two. That is the question the field exists to answer. The elements were not distributed at random, and the pattern is chemical.

  Victor Goldschmidt gave the explanation in the 1920s. The forming Earth separated into a metallic core, a sulphide layer, a silicate mantle and an atmosphere, and each element went where its chemistry sent it: those that prefer to bond with oxygen stayed in the silicate, those that prefer sulphur went with the sulphides, those that dissolve in molten iron sank. Gold is rare at the surface not because the planet lacks it but because gold prefers metal and the metal is four thousand kilometres down. The Earth is a partitioning experiment that ran once.

  What makes the field more than descriptive is that the proportions carry information. Harold Urey showed in 1947 that the ratio of oxygen isotopes locked into a carbonate depends on the temperature at which it crystallised, so a shell is a thermometer. Sulphur isotopes in ancient rocks date the arrival of atmospheric oxygen. And measuring any of this at the required precision turned out to demand a discipline of its own — Clair Patterson's pursuit of contamination, undertaken to get one number right, incidentally established that the twentieth-century world was pervasively poisoned with lead.

key_ideas:
  - term: Crustal abundance
    definition: >-
      The average concentration of an element in the Earth's crust, against which any rock or ore is judged.
      Oxygen and silicon alone are nearly three-quarters of it by mass; an ore body is simply somewhere the
      local figure is a few orders of magnitude above the average.
    turning_point_id: clarke-crustal-abundances
  - term: Geochemical affinity
    definition: >-
      Goldschmidt's classification of the elements by what they prefer to bond with: lithophile for oxygen and
      silicate, chalcophile for sulphur, siderophile for metallic iron, atmophile for the gas phase. It
      predicts which reservoir of the planet an element is in.
    turning_point_id: goldschmidt-element-classification
  - term: Isotope fractionation
    definition: >-
      The slight preference of a physical or chemical process for one isotope over another, because mass
      affects vibrational energies and diffusion rates. The effect is a fraction of a per cent, which is why
      it is useful: it is small enough to be reversible and large enough to measure.
    turning_point_id: urey-isotope-thermometer
  - term: Silicate weathering feedback
    definition: >-
      Carbon dioxide dissolves in rain, the acid dissolves silicate rock, and the products are carried to the
      sea and buried as carbonate. The reaction speeds up when it is warmer and wetter, so it removes CO₂
      faster the more there is — a thermostat with a response time of hundreds of thousands of years.
    turning_point_id: silicate-weathering-thermostat
  - term: Procedural blank
    definition: >-
      What the measurement reports when there is no sample: the contamination contributed by the reagents,
      the vessels, the air and the analyst. For a trace element it is often larger than the quantity sought,
      and no result means anything until it is smaller.
    turning_point_id: patterson-lead-contamination
  - term: Mass-independent fractionation
    definition: >-
      An isotope pattern that does not scale with mass difference, and therefore cannot have been produced by
      ordinary chemistry. In sulphur it is a photochemical signature, and its disappearance from the rock
      record marks the moment the atmosphere acquired an ozone layer.
    turning_point_id: great-oxidation-from-isotopes

## Eight Elements and Everything Else

{{fig:frank-clarke|Frank Wigglesworth Clarke}} analysed rocks for the United States Geological Survey for thirty years and turned the averages into a table. The result is still the first thing anyone learns about the Earth's chemistry:

| Element | Per cent of crust by mass |
| --- | --- |
| Oxygen | 46 |
| Silicon | 28 |
| Aluminium | 8 |
| Iron | 6 |
| Calcium, sodium, potassium, magnesium | ~9 together |
| **Everything else** | **~2** |

Eight elements, ninety-eight per cent. Oxygen and silicon alone are nearly three-quarters, so to a first approximation the crust is silicate and the rest is detail. Copper is 60 parts per million, lead 14, gold about 4 parts per billion.

Two questions follow immediately, and the field is largely the history of answering them. Why those elements and not others — a question about the chemistry of a differentiating planet. And why, if gold averages 4 parts per billion, there are places where it is present at a thousand times that and can be mined — a question about how the chemistry concentrates things locally. An ore deposit stopped being a thing one finds and became an anomaly requiring an explanation.

## The Planet as a Partitioning Experiment

{{fig:goldschmidt|Victor Goldschmidt}} supplied the global answer in the 1920s and 1930s, and it is one idea applied consistently.

A molten planet containing iron, sulphur, silicate and gas separates into those four phases, and each element distributes itself between them according to which it prefers — a partition coefficient, exactly as in a solvent extraction. Goldschmidt sorted the periodic table accordingly:

- **Siderophile**: dissolves in molten iron. Nickel, cobalt, gold, platinum, iridium.
- **Chalcophile**: goes with sulphur. Copper, zinc, lead, silver, mercury.
- **Lithophile**: bonds to oxygen, stays in the silicate. Aluminium, the alkalis, the rare earths, uranium.
- **Atmophile**: stays as gas. Nitrogen, the noble gases, hydrogen.

The explanatory payoff is large. Gold is not rare in the Earth; it is rare *at the surface*, because gold prefers metal and almost all the metal is in a core four thousand kilometres down. The platinum-group metals that catalysis depends on are scarce for the same reason, which is a geochemical fact underlying the economic one noted in [organometallic chemistry](/chemistry/organometallic-chemistry/). Uranium and thorium are lithophile, so they stayed in the silicate — which is why radioactive heating warms the mantle rather than the core, and why the Earth is still tectonically active.

Goldschmidt's other major contribution, the set of ionic radii and the rules for which ion substitutes for which in a crystal, is taken up in [solid-state chemistry](/chemistry/solid-state-chemistry/); here the point is the reverse one. Because substitution follows radius and charge, a trace element tracks a major one, and the ratio between them records what the rock has been through.

## Rocks That Record a Temperature and a Date

The field's second instrument is the isotope ratio, and it works because the effects are small.

{{fig:urey|Harold Urey}} calculated in 1947 that the oxygen isotopes in a carbonate are distributed slightly differently from those in the water it grew from, and that the difference depends on temperature — by a few parts in ten thousand per degree. {{fig:epstein|Samuel Epstein}} calibrated it on shells grown at known temperatures. A fossil shell is therefore a thermometer, and almost everything quantitative that is known about the Earth's temperature history descends from this one calculation, through foraminifera in sea-floor mud, ice in Greenland, and layers in a cave.

{{fig:urey|Urey}} also noticed the planet's thermostat. Carbon dioxide dissolves in rain, the weak acid dissolves silicate rock, and the dissolved products are carried to the sea where organisms bury them as carbonate — so weathering is a drain on atmospheric carbon dioxide. {{fig:james-walker|James Walker}}, {{fig:paul-hays|Paul Hays}} and {{fig:kasting|James Kasting}} added the feedback in 1981: the reaction runs faster when it is warmer and wetter, so the drain opens wider the more carbon dioxide there is. The Sun has brightened by roughly a third over the Earth's history and the surface has stayed within the range where water is liquid, which this feedback is the best available explanation for.

The sharpest piece of dating came last. {{fig:cloud|Preston Cloud}} and {{fig:heinrich-holland|Heinrich Holland}} argued from the rocks that the early atmosphere contained almost no oxygen — banded iron formations require dissolved iron, which needs anoxic water, and they give way to oxidised red beds at a definite horizon. {{fig:farquhar|James Farquhar}} found the decisive marker in 2000. Sulphur isotopes in rocks older than about 2.4 billion years are fractionated in a way that does not scale with mass, which ordinary chemical reactions cannot produce and ultraviolet photochemistry can. The pattern vanishes at 2.4 billion years, which is when an ozone layer appeared to block the ultraviolet — so a measurement of sulphur in rock dates the oxygenation of the atmosphere, and in doing so hands the problem to [atmospheric chemistry](/chemistry/atmospheric-chemistry/).

## A Closer Look: A Number Is Only as Good as Its Blank

{{fig:clair-patterson|Clair Patterson}} set out in the 1950s to measure lead isotopes in meteorites precisely enough to date the Earth, and found that he could not, because everything he touched contained lead. What followed is the clearest case in chemistry of a measurement limit being a scientific result in itself.

Consider the arithmetic. The lead concentration of deep Pacific water is a few thousandths of a microgram per kilogram. To measure that in a one-kilogram sample is to measure a few nanograms of lead in total. Now consider the laboratory. Urban air in the 1960s carried on the order of one microgram of lead per cubic metre, which is **one nanogram per litre of air**. A single litre of room air therefore contains as much lead as the entire sample.

Everything follows from that comparison. Reagent acids had to be distilled in-house, because commercial acid carried more lead than the sample. Vessels had to be leached for weeks, because glass and even plastic release lead. Air had to be filtered and the laboratory held at positive pressure. Analysts had to abandon cosmetics and hair treatments. Patterson reduced his procedural blank — what the method reports when there is no sample at all — by something like four orders of magnitude, and only then were the numbers meaningful.

And the numbers were not small corrections to what had been published. They were lower by one to two orders of magnitude. Every earlier measurement of lead in seawater had been measuring the laboratory.

Then came the consequence Patterson had not been looking for. If uncontaminated natural lead concentrations are a hundredth of what everyone assumed, then the modern environment is not naturally leaded — it is polluted, and by a factor nobody had suspected. Patterson tested it on an archive that could not have been contaminated at the time of deposition: the Greenland ice sheet, layer by layer.

| Ice from | Lead (µg per kg) |
| --- | --- |
| ~800 BC | ~0.001 |
| 1750 | ~0.01 |
| 1965 | ~0.2 |

A **two-hundredfold** rise, with most of it in the few decades after tetraethyl lead was introduced as a petrol additive. The claim was contested for twenty years by scientists funded by the industry that made the additive, on the grounds that lead at these levels was natural and harmless. Patterson argued it with measurements, lost funding over it, and was left off the relevant national advisory panel.

The outcome is in the public health record. Leaded petrol was phased out in the United States through the late 1970s and 1980s, and the mean blood lead concentration of American children fell from about **16 micrograms per decilitre** at the end of the 1970s to under **1** today — the two curves, fuel lead sold and blood lead measured, tracking each other closely enough that they are usually plotted on the same axes. The global phase-out completed in 2021.

The methodological lesson generalised. Every trace-element measurement in environmental chemistry now reports its blank, because the field learned here that an unexamined blank does not produce an imprecise answer — it produces a confident wrong one, and a wrong baseline makes a pollutant look natural.

## Reading the Planet's Chemistry

Geochemistry's position in this thread is as the discipline that establishes what normal is. A contaminant can only be identified as one against a baseline; a climate change can only be measured against a record; an ore body is an anomaly relative to a crustal average. All three of those reference points are analytical chemistry applied to rock, ice and water, and the lead story is the standing demonstration that getting the baseline wrong is worse than having none.

Two directions lead out. The atmosphere is the reservoir whose chemistry turns over fastest — days rather than millions of years — and is taken up in [atmospheric chemistry](/chemistry/atmospheric-chemistry/). The ocean is where the weathering products arrive and where the carbonate system buffers the whole surface Earth, which is [aquatic chemistry](/chemistry/aquatic-chemistry/). The field's own fog is on either side of what it can sample: the composition of the deep planet, which no instrument reaches, and the strength of the weathering feedback, which is averaged over every rock on the surface and measured only through the rivers that drain them.
