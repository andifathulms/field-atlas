core_question: What cleans the atmosphere, and how long does a molecule released into it last?

summary: |-
  Several billion tonnes of reduced gas enter the atmosphere every year — methane from wetlands and livestock, carbon monoxide from fires and engines, hydrocarbons from vegetation — and the atmosphere does not fill up with them. Something removes them, and it is not oxygen, which is thermodynamically capable and kinetically hopeless: oxygen cannot attack a saturated hydrocarbon at ambient temperature at any measurable rate.

  Hiram Levy identified the agent in 1971, by calculation rather than by measurement. A small fraction of sunlight is energetic enough to split ozone into an excited oxygen atom, and that atom reacts with water vapour to give two hydroxyl radicals. The resulting concentration is about a million radicals per cubic centimetre — four parts in a hundred trillion, a mixing ratio so low it was for decades unmeasurable — and because hydroxyl attacks almost everything with a hydrogen atom on it, that is enough to set the lifetime of nearly every reduced gas in the air.

  The field's organising quantity is therefore the lifetime, and it follows from a rate constant and one concentration. Carbon monoxide lasts two months, methane about eleven years, and a chlorofluorocarbon lasts a century — not because it is stable in any absolute sense but because it has no hydrogen for hydroxyl to take, so the atmosphere has no way to begin. The gases that turned out to be environmental problems are, almost without exception, the ones designed to be unreactive.

key_ideas:
  - term: Atmospheric lifetime
    definition: >-
      The total amount of a gas in the atmosphere divided by the rate at which it is removed. For a gas
      destroyed by a single reaction it is one over the rate constant times the oxidant concentration, which
      makes it computable from two numbers.
    turning_point_id: global-oh-from-tracers
  - term: Catalytic cycle
    definition: >-
      A sequence in which a trace species destroys a major one and is regenerated, so that one molecule of
      catalyst accounts for thousands of destructions. It is why a gas present at parts per billion can control
      the budget of one present at parts per million.
    turning_point_id: bates-nicolet-catalytic-cycles
  - term: The hydroxyl radical
    definition: >-
      OH, generated by sunlight acting on ozone and water, present at roughly a million per cubic centimetre
      and surviving under a second. It is the principal oxidant of the troposphere and the reason the
      atmosphere is self-cleaning.
    turning_point_id: levy-hydroxyl-radical
  - term: Photolysis rate
    definition: >-
      How often a molecule is split by sunlight per second, written as a $J$ value. It depends on the
      molecule's absorption spectrum overlapping the solar spectrum that reaches that altitude, which is why
      the chemistry of the stratosphere and the troposphere differ so completely.
    turning_point_id: chapman-ozone-layer
  - term: Secondary pollutant
    definition: >-
      A substance not emitted but manufactured in the air from things that were. Most of the ozone and most of
      the fine particulate matter people breathe is secondary, which means the thing to regulate is not the
      thing being measured.
    turning_point_id: secondary-organic-aerosol
  - term: Global warming potential
    definition: >-
      The heat trapped by a mass of a gas over a chosen period, relative to the same mass of carbon dioxide.
      It is mostly a lifetime in disguise, which is why the figure for methane changes by a factor of three
      depending on whether twenty or a hundred years is chosen.
    turning_point_id: global-oh-from-tracers

## Why the Atmosphere Is Not Full of Everything Ever Emitted

Wetlands, livestock, rice paddies and leaking gas infrastructure put several hundred million tonnes of methane into the air each year. Fires and engines add several hundred million tonnes of carbon monoxide. Forests emit a comparable mass of volatile hydrocarbons — isoprene alone is around five hundred million tonnes a year, more than all human hydrocarbon emissions combined. None of it accumulates indefinitely. Something takes it out.

Not oxygen. Oxygen makes up a fifth of the atmosphere and is thermodynamically more than capable of burning methane, but it does not, because the reaction is spin-forbidden and has a large barrier: a mixture of methane and air sits indefinitely at room temperature, which is why natural gas can be stored. Thermodynamics permits and kinetics forbids, which is the distinction [chemical kinetics](/chemistry/chemical-kinetics/) exists to make.

So the question is what reactive species the atmosphere makes for itself, and the answer begins with light.

{{fig:chapman|Sydney Chapman}} did the first such calculation in 1930, for ozone. Ultraviolet light splits molecular oxygen; the atoms add to O₂ to make ozone; ozone absorbs longer ultraviolet and is split back. Four reactions, all involving nothing but oxygen, and the scheme explains why ozone forms a layer at a particular height — high enough for the ultraviolet to penetrate, low enough for the gas density to allow three-body combination. It was the first time the composition of an atmosphere had been computed from reaction rates rather than described.

It also gives about twice too much ozone. The error mattered more than the agreement, because explaining it took the field to its central idea.

## A Trace Species Controlling a Major One

{{fig:bates|David Bates}} and {{fig:nicolet|Marcel Nicolet}} proposed in 1950 that the missing loss process is catalytic. A hydrogen radical, derived from the small amount of water vapour present, reacts with ozone; the product reacts with an oxygen atom; and the radical comes back unchanged, ready to do it again. One radical can destroy thousands of ozone molecules.

This is leverage, and the leverage is the point. The ozone layer contains ozone at a few parts per million; the radicals destroying it are present at parts per trillion. A constituent six orders of magnitude rarer governs the budget of one six orders of magnitude more abundant, because catalysis decouples the amount of a species from the amount of work it does. It follows that the composition of an atmosphere is not controlled by its major constituents, and that a very small addition can have a very large effect — which is the whole argument of the two fields that follow this one.

{{fig:crutzen|Paul Crutzen}} supplied the most striking instance in 1970. Nitric oxide and nitrogen dioxide form a catalytic cycle destroying ozone, and they are the dominant natural loss in the middle stratosphere. Their source is not industrial: soil bacteria produce nitrous oxide, which is unreactive in the lower atmosphere, drifts upward over decades, and is converted where the ultraviolet is strong enough. The thickness of a layer thirty kilometres up is therefore set in part by microbial metabolism in soil. Crutzen shared the 1995 Nobel Prize for this work.

## The Detergent

The lower atmosphere needed its own oxidant, and {{fig:levy|Hiram Levy}} found it in 1971 in a two-page paper, by calculation, before anyone could measure it.

The short-wavelength tail of the sunlight reaching the troposphere is just energetic enough to split ozone into molecular oxygen and an **electronically excited** oxygen atom. An ordinary oxygen atom would simply re-form ozone; the excited one has enough energy to react with water vapour, producing two hydroxyl radicals. Hydroxyl then abstracts a hydrogen atom from almost anything that has one.

The resulting concentration is about a million radicals per cubic centimetre. Air at sea level contains $2.5 \times 10^{19}$ molecules per cubic centimetre, so the mixing ratio is

$$
\frac{10^6}{2.5 \times 10^{19}} = 4 \times 10^{-14},
$$

four parts in a hundred trillion. A single hydroxyl radical per twenty-five trillion air molecules, each surviving well under a second, cleans the atmosphere.

Which raises an obvious difficulty: how is a number like that established? Not by direct measurement — local measurements are possible now and were not then, and a global average of a species that varies with the sunlight is not something a few instruments can supply. {{fig:prinn|Ronald Prinn}} and colleagues did it backwards. Methyl chloroform was an industrial solvent whose annual production was known from company records, and whose only significant atmospheric sink is reaction with hydroxyl. Measure its global burden and its rate of decline, subtract the known emissions, and the loss rate falls out; divide by the laboratory rate constant and what remains is the global mean hydroxyl concentration. An industrial chemical used as a deliberate tracer for a radical that cannot be seen.

The other half of modern atmospheric chemistry is not in the gas phase at all. The fine particles that dominate urban air pollution are mostly not emitted as particles: hydrocarbons are oxidised through many steps into products of low volatility, which condense. {{fig:pankow|James Pankow}} provided the partitioning framework, and single-particle mass spectrometry showed the organic fraction to be an enormous mixture of oxidation products, much of it continuing to react inside the particle. The subject acquired a condensed phase, and with it the two open problems recorded above.

## A Closer Look: What Sets a Molecule's Lifetime

For a gas removed by one reaction with an oxidant at concentration $[X]$, the lifetime is

$$
\tau = \frac{1}{k[X]}.
$$

Two numbers. Take methane, oxidised by hydroxyl. The rate constant is

$$
k = 2.45 \times 10^{-12}\,e^{-1775/T}\ \text{cm}^3\,\text{molecule}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1},
$$

and at the mass-weighted mean temperature of the troposphere where the reaction happens, about 272 K,

$$
k = 2.45 \times 10^{-12} \times e^{-6.53} = 2.45 \times 10^{-12} \times 1.46 \times 10^{-3} = 3.6 \times 10^{-15}.
$$

With $[\mathrm{OH}] = 1.0 \times 10^6$ cm⁻³,

$$
\tau = \frac{1}{3.6 \times 10^{-15} \times 10^{6}} = 2.8 \times 10^{8}\ \text{s} = 9\ \text{years}.
$$

The observationally constrained figure for methane's loss to hydroxyl is about eleven years. For an estimate using one rate constant and one concentration, that is close, and the remaining difference is mostly that some of the methane is in colder air where the reaction is slower still.

Now the same calculation for other gases, and the spread is the whole subject:

| Gas | Removed by | $k$ at 272 K (cm³ s⁻¹) | Lifetime |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carbon monoxide | OH | $2.4 \times 10^{-13}$ | ~2 months |
| Methane | OH | $3.6 \times 10^{-15}$ | ~11 years |
| CFC-12 | nothing in the troposphere | — | ~100 years |
| SF₆ | nothing below the mesosphere | — | ~3,200 years |

Carbon monoxide and methane differ by a factor of seventy in rate constant and therefore in lifetime: one is a regional pollutant, the other a global greenhouse gas, and the difference is one Arrhenius exponent.

The bottom two rows are the important ones, and they are not a continuation of the trend. CFC-12 is CF₂Cl₂. It has **no hydrogen atom**, so the one reaction hydroxyl performs — abstracting a hydrogen — is unavailable, and it does not absorb in the solar spectrum that reaches the lower atmosphere. The troposphere has no mechanism to begin on it. The molecule simply circulates, mixing through the whole atmosphere over a year or two, until it eventually reaches altitudes above 25 kilometres where the ultraviolet is short enough to break a C–Cl bond. That journey takes about a century, and what happens on arrival is the subject of [stratospheric ozone](/chemistry/stratospheric-ozone/). Sulphur hexafluoride, chosen as an insulator for exactly its inertness as noted in [main-group chemistry](/chemistry/main-group-chemistry/), lasts thirty times longer again.

So there is a pattern, and it is uncomfortable. The gases that became environmental problems were, almost without exception, selected by industry *for being unreactive* — as refrigerants that would not burn or poison, as solvents that would not degrade, as insulators that would not break down. Non-flammable, non-toxic and chemically inert is a specification an engineer writes and an atmospheric chemist reads as a long lifetime, because the atmosphere's only tool is a radical that attacks hydrogen atoms.

One further consequence closes the arithmetic. Because global warming potential is the heat trapped integrated over a chosen period, and because the integral of a decaying quantity is dominated by how long it lasts, a lifetime becomes a price. Methane's potential is about 84 over twenty years and about 28 over a hundred — the same molecule, a factor of three apart, because most of it has gone by the end of the longer window. Which horizon a treaty writes into its text therefore changes what a tonne of methane is worth, and the number came out of a rate constant measured in a flask.

## What the Atmosphere Cannot Clean

The field arrives at a fairly clean statement of its own scope. The atmosphere is a photochemical reactor with one principal reagent, generated by sunlight and consumed within a second, present at four parts in a hundred trillion. What it can attack has a lifetime of days to years. What it cannot attack has a lifetime of centuries, and reaches places it was never meant to go.

Two of those places are the subject of the fields that follow. In the polluted lower atmosphere, hydroxyl chemistry running in the presence of nitrogen oxides manufactures ozone and particles at ground level instead of destroying them, which is [photochemical smog](/chemistry/photochemical-smog/). In the stratosphere, molecules that survived the troposphere are finally broken apart and release catalysts into the one place where ozone matters most, which is [stratospheric ozone](/chemistry/stratospheric-ozone/).

What the field cannot yet do is account for everything that is there. The measured rate at which air consumes hydroxyl exceeds the rate calculated from all identified compounds by thirty to fifty per cent, over forests and in cities alike — so a substantial fraction of what is reacting in the atmosphere has never been identified. That is a different problem from the one recorded under [chemical kinetics](/chemistry/chemical-kinetics/), which concerns uncertain rate constants in a known mechanism; here the mechanism itself is missing species.
