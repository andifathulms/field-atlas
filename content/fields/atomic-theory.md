core_question: If substances combine in fixed proportions by weight, how heavy is one particle of each — and how can that be known without ever seeing one?

summary: |-
  John Dalton's contribution was not the idea of atoms, which is ancient, but a way to put numbers on them. If a compound always contains the same proportions by mass, and if two elements that form several compounds do so in ratios of small whole numbers, then matter comes in indivisible units with characteristic weights, and those weights can be computed from analyses. He published a table in 1808. Almost every value in it was wrong, and the method was right.

  What went wrong is instructive. To get an atomic weight from a mass proportion, you must know how many atoms of each kind are in the molecule, and nothing in the analysis tells you. Dalton assumed the simplest formula available — water as one hydrogen and one oxygen — and so halved oxygen's weight. Gay-Lussac's measurement that gases combine in simple volume ratios contained the missing information, and Avogadro drew the correct conclusion in 1811: equal volumes hold equal numbers of molecules, and the common gases are diatomic. Dalton rejected it. The field spent fifty years with two incompatible sets of atomic weights in circulation, until Stanislao Cannizzaro showed in 1858 how to use Avogadro's hypothesis systematically, and a congress at Karlsruhe handed his pamphlet out at the door.

key_ideas:
  - term: Law of multiple proportions
    definition: >-
      When two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element combining with a fixed
      mass of the other stand in ratios of small whole numbers — 1:2 for carbon monoxide and dioxide.
      Whole numbers are what indivisible units produce.
    turning_point_id: dalton-atomic-theory
  - term: Atomic weight
    definition: >-
      The mass of an atom relative to a standard, not in grams: chemistry could rank and compare
      particle masses for a century before it could weigh one. Hydrogen, then oxygen, then carbon-12
      served as the standard.
    turning_point_id: dalton-atomic-theory
  - term: Combining volumes
    definition: >-
      Gases react in ratios of simple whole numbers *by volume* as well as by mass — two volumes of
      hydrogen with one of oxygen. The regularity is about counting particles, and it is visible
      without any weighing at all.
    turning_point_id: gay-lussac-combining-volumes
  - term: Avogadro's hypothesis
    definition: >-
      Equal volumes of any gas at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of
      molecules. It converts a volume ratio into a molecular formula, and it requires that hydrogen
      and oxygen gas be pairs of atoms rather than single ones.
    turning_point_id: avogadro-hypothesis
  - term: Atom against molecule
    definition: >-
      The distinction Dalton lacked: the smallest particle of an element is not necessarily the
      smallest particle that exists free. Without it, vapour densities and combining volumes
      contradict each other.
    turning_point_id: karlsruhe-cannizzaro
  - term: Stoichiometry
    definition: >-
      Computing the quantities consumed and produced in a reaction from its balanced equation. It is
      the whole practical content of atomic theory, and the reason a chemical plant can be designed
      on paper.
    turning_point_id: berzelius-atomic-weights

## Whole Numbers Where None Were Expected

By 1800 chemistry had the law of definite proportions: a compound's composition is fixed. {{fig:dalton|John Dalton}} noticed something sharper. Where two elements form more than one compound, the proportions are related by small whole numbers. Carbon forms two oxides, and the oxygen combining with a fixed weight of carbon in one is exactly twice that in the other. Nitrogen forms several, and the ratios are 1 : 2 : 3 : 4 : 5.

Continuous matter has no reason to behave like that. Indivisible units do: a molecule contains one oxygen atom or two, never one and a half. Dalton drew the conclusion, and then drew out its consequence — if atoms have characteristic weights, an analysis can be inverted to find them.

His 1808 table is the first attempt, and it is wrong almost throughout. The reason is a gap in the method rather than in the measurements. Analysis gives the *proportion by mass*; converting it to an atomic weight requires knowing the formula, and nothing in the analysis supplies one. Dalton adopted a rule of greatest simplicity: water, having one known composition, should be one atom of each. Water is 11.2% hydrogen by mass, so on that assumption oxygen weighs about eight times hydrogen. The true figure is sixteen, and every weight derived through a wrong formula inherited the error.

## The Information Was Already There

The missing information arrived the same year, from {{fig:gay-lussac|Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac}}, and was rejected. Gases, he found, combine in volume ratios of small whole numbers, and the product's volume joins the pattern:

$$
2 \text{ volumes } \mathrm{H} + 1 \text{ volume } \mathrm{O} \longrightarrow 2 \text{ volumes of steam}.
$$

Volumes are counts of particles if equal volumes hold equal numbers, so this is a formula written in the laboratory. Dalton would not have it. On his picture the particles in a volume of oxygen are single atoms, so making *two* volumes of product from *one* of oxygen requires each oxygen atom to be split between two particles of water — which contradicts indivisibility. Faced with a measurement that broke his theory, he doubted the measurement.

{{fig:avogadro|Amedeo Avogadro}} resolved it in 1811 with two propositions that have to be taken together. Equal volumes of any gas contain equal numbers of molecules; and the molecules of an elementary gas may contain more than one atom. If oxygen gas is $\mathrm{O_2}$, one volume of it supplies two oxygen atoms, no atom is divided, water is $\mathrm{H_2O}$, and oxygen weighs sixteen. The paper was noticed and set aside as metaphysics, partly because Avogadro's terminology was obscure, partly because {{fig:berzelius|Berzelius}}'s rival electrochemical theory of combination had better empirical credit, and partly because the hypothesis cannot be tested except through the atomic weights it produces.

So chemistry proceeded for fifty years with two systems of weights, the choice between them a matter of school and nationality. The same compound had different formulas in different journals.

## A Closer Look: How Cannizzaro Broke the Circle

{{fig:cannizzaro|Stanislao Cannizzaro}}'s 1858 pamphlet is a procedure, and its merit is that it is self-checking. Two steps.

**Step one: molecular weights from vapour densities.** Measure the density of a vapour relative to hydrogen gas by {{fig:dumas|Dumas}}'s method. Equal volumes hold equal numbers of molecules, so the density ratio is the ratio of molecular weights. Setting hydrogen gas at 2 — because it is $\mathrm{H_2}$ — gives the molecular weight directly as twice the relative density.

**Step two: atomic weights from the smallest share.** Analyse each compound for its percentage of an element. Multiplying by the molecular weight gives the mass of that element present in one molecule. Then take the atomic weight to be the *smallest* such mass found in any compound, because no molecule can contain less than one atom.

Carry it out for carbon:

| Compound | Molecular weight | Carbon by mass | Carbon per molecule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide | 44.0 | 27.3% | 12.0 |
| Carbon monoxide | 28.0 | 42.9% | 12.0 |
| Methane | 16.0 | 74.9% | 12.0 |
| Ethane | 30.1 | 79.9% | 24.0 |
| Ethanol | 46.1 | 52.1% | 24.0 |

The column on the right contains 12.0 and 24.0 and nothing between them. The atomic weight of carbon is therefore 12, and ethane and ethanol contain two atoms of it. Do the same for oxygen and the smallest share is 16, never 8; for hydrogen, 1.

Check one entry by hand. Carbon dioxide's molecular weight is 44.0, and carbon is $12.011/44.01 = 27.3\%$ of it, so the carbon in one molecule weighs $0.273 \times 44.0 = 12.0$. For ethanol, $\mathrm{C_2H_6O}$, molecular weight 46.07 and carbon $2 \times 12.011/46.07 = 52.1\%$, giving $0.521 \times 46.1 = 24.0$ — two atoms.

The power of the procedure is in its redundancy. Any one compound leaves the formula ambiguous; a dozen compounds of the same element cannot all be consistent with the wrong atomic weight. And the method exposes its own failures: sulfur's vapour density gives a molecular weight near 256 at low temperature and near 64 at high, which under Avogadro means $\mathrm{S_8}$ dissociating to $\mathrm{S_2}$. Dumas had taken the same anomaly as evidence against the hypothesis. Read through the hypothesis, it is a measurement of molecular structure.

At the Karlsruhe congress in September 1860 — the first international meeting of chemists, convened precisely because the atomic weights were in disarray — no resolution was passed. Copies of Cannizzaro's pamphlet were handed out as the delegates left. Lothar Meyer later wrote that reading it on the journey home felt as though the scales fell from his eyes, and within a few years he and {{fig:mendeleev|Mendeleev}} had a consistent set of weights to arrange — which is where the [periodic system](/chemistry/periodic-system/) begins.

## Relative Until Physics Arrived

One feature of this whole episode deserves a note, because it is easy to miss from the modern side. Every weight in Cannizzaro's table is *relative*. Chemistry ranked and compared the masses of particles for a century without the least idea how many particles were in a gram, and it did not need one: a balanced equation only ever uses ratios.

The absolute number came from outside. {{fig:jean-perrin|Jean Perrin}}'s measurements of Brownian particles — how they settle in a column, how far they wander — gave Avogadro's constant to within a few per cent and were widely taken as the end of the argument about whether atoms are real or a useful fiction, as [kinetic theory](/physics/kinetic-theory/) describes. Chemistry had been doing exact arithmetic on objects whose existence was still in dispute, which is a reasonable summary of how the subject got to 1900.

The inheritance is the mole, and a last twist. An atomic weight is an average over isotopes, and that average depends on where the sample was dug up. Since 2009, the standard atomic weights of fourteen elements have been published not as values but as intervals — sulfur lies somewhere between 32.059 and 32.076 — because for those elements there is no universal constant to be measured, only a range that provenance selects from.
